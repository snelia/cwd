“Every Mother a Missionary”?: The Vlogs of Mormon Mothers

Spring 2025 Religious Studies Capstone Paper

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS Church) places high expectations on mothers to rear children into godly adults. Mormon mothers on Instagram circumvent religious expectations through vlogs by marketing, withholding information about their Mormon faith, and scarcely showing their husbands on-camera. Through descriptive qualitative coding, this study categorizes the roles of husbands, children, and online marketing in the vlog content of ten Mormon mothers on Instagram. The coding process incorporated the ten most recent videos on each account as of March 21, 2025, totaling at 100 posts coded. In only 9% of videos, husbands were present and cared for, while children were present and cared for in 33% of videos. Supplementing the absence of family, 53% of the videos contain advertisement in the form of endorsements, affiliate links, or self-promotion: all ten accounts earn commission on products and/or sell their own products at least once within this study. Finally, only 2% of videos mentioned Mormon religious beliefs. Mormon mother influencers omit their husbands and religion where possible to monetize themselves independently of their family members, become marketable to a broader audience, and subvert expectations of the LDS Church.

Qualitative Coding

I utilized descriptive qualitative coding with a deductive approach, surveying relevant LDS Church publications (speeches and The Ensign of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints) to develop my codes. The final codes categorize visibility of husbands and children with four degrees: present, cared for; present, background; absent, mentioned; absent, not mentioned. The “cared for” code involves physical displays of affection (i.e. hugging, kissing, and holding hands), verbal praise, purchases made for the others, and tasks done with the husband on-camera that have the express purpose of fulfilling the others’ needs. In coding the presence of husbands and children, new trends emerged to characterize an inductive data set: marketing. This process resulted in four codes: no marketing, endorsement, affiliate links, and self-promotion. The “endorsement” code involves sponsorships or partnerships with companies, and affiliate links involve affiliate IDs and UTM data.

Husbands

Despite the very broad operational definition of “cared for,” husbands were cared for in only 9% of the videos, and in the background during 18% of videos. A faithful marriage is regarded as the precursor to achieving the highest level of glory as an LDS Church member, but rearing children took precedence in online spaces. The husbands’ social media accounts tend to feature affection and care between spouses that is not otherwise visible on the mother’s account, indicating the deliberate curation by Mormon mothers on their accounts.

Children

With the same criteria, children were cared for in 33% of the videos. While children were still absent from over half of the posts, they were 148% more present in the videos than husbands were. For added context, the sampled Mormon mothers had a mean average of 4.2 children. The data was highly polarized, with “cared for” and “not mentioned” constituting all but 13% of the codes. Still, the theme of marketing emerged in greater proportion than any of the “present” codes, setting aside a space for these Mormon mothers to become breadwinners.

Marketing

One of the more common strategies in marketing exercised by these Mormon mothers was link cloaking. Link cloaking techniques hide both the destination site and advertising information through URL shortening websites like TinyURL, Bitly, and many others. Meta has policies opposing link cloaking, but disguised links were still quite visible on influencers’ Instagram accounts. Affiliate status necessitated finding the links, opening them, and examining the URL for affiliate IDs that give the influencer commission and/or Urchin Traffic Monitor (UTM) strings that provide marketing analytics.
